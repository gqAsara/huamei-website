# US import tariffs on Chinese packaging in 2026: what brands sourcing rigid boxes need to know

By Sonia Sun, Founder, Huamei 華美 — since 1992. Published 23 May 2026. Updated 23 May 2026.

Sonia Sun has managed export packaging for US and European brands from Huamei's factories in Henan, Zhejiang, Sichuan, and Guizhou since founding the company in 1992 — including shipments subject to the Section 301 tariff schedule on Chinese goods.

Rigid boxes and luxury packaging imported from China to the United States are subject to additional duties under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974. These tariffs, layered on top of the standard Most-Favoured-Nation duty rate, have been in force since 2018–2019 and have seen further changes since 2025. For US brands sourcing gift boxes, rigid presentation cases, and specialty packaging from Chinese manufacturers, the tariff calculus is now part of every sourcing decision. This guide covers the HTS classification of rigid packaging, the applicable tariff frameworks, practical cost implications, and strategies US brands use to manage duty exposure.

## What HTS code applies to rigid paperboard boxes from China?

> Luxury rigid paperboard boxes (set-up, non-collapsible) from China are typically classified under HTS 4819.20 or a set-up box subheading under Chapter 48. The standard Most-Favoured-Nation duty rate under Chapter 48 is 0–5.5%; Section 301 additional duties stack on top and must be verified against the current USTR tariff schedule.

HTS classification is the foundation of any tariff calculation. Luxury rigid gift boxes — the kind produced at Huamei in 2.0–3.0 mm greyboard with laminate, [hot-foil](/craft/rigid), and emboss finishes — are classified under Chapter 48 (paper and paperboard articles), specifically HTS heading 4819. The exact subheading depends on construction:

- **HTS 4819.10** — Cartons, boxes, and cases of corrugated paper or paperboard (folded flat or shipped assembled)
- **HTS 4819.20** — Folding cartons, boxes, and cases of non-corrugated paper or paperboard
- **HTS 4819.50** — Other packing containers of paper or paperboard, which may capture set-up (non-collapsible) rigid boxes shipped pre-formed

Most luxury rigid gift boxes — assembled, glued, and shipped in a pre-formed state — are properly classified as set-up boxes. Misclassification between 4819.20 and 4819.50 is common and can result in either underpayment of duty (a compliance risk) or overpayment (a recoverable cost). For orders above $50,000 in customs value, a formal Binding Ruling from [US Customs and Border Protection](https://www.cbp.gov/trade/rulings) costs nothing and eliminates the classification risk; the ruling process takes three to six weeks.

## What Section 301 tariffs apply to Chinese packaging?

Section 301 tariffs were first imposed in 2018–2019 across four lists covering essentially all goods imported from China, including paper and board packaging under Chapter 48. The [Office of the United States Trade Representative (USTR)](https://ustr.gov/issue-areas/enforcement/section-301-investigations/tariff-actions) publishes the authoritative and current tariff schedule; rates have been subject to change since 2025 and brands should verify the current applicable rate before placing a production order.

Section 301 additional duties stack on top of the standard column 1 MFN rate. For HTS 4819 subheadings, the MFN rate is typically 0–5.5% depending on exact subheading. The Section 301 additional duty brings total effective rates substantially higher. The authoritative source for current applicable rates is the [HTS schedule maintained by the USITC](https://hts.usitc.gov/), which is updated to reflect current tariff actions. Any specific rate stated in secondary sources (including this article) should be verified against the USITC HTS at the time of import.

The practical implication: a luxury rigid box with an FOB factory value of $3.00 per unit (typical for a 200-piece premium gift box from a Chinese manufacturer) will carry an additional duty charge per unit. At scale, this is a material cost that belongs in the landed cost model alongside freight, insurance, and last-mile distribution.

## What cost strategies do US brands use to manage packaging duty exposure?

Several legitimate strategies reduce the effective duty cost on packaging imported from China.

**First sale valuation.** US customs duties are normally assessed on the last sale price — the price paid to the Chinese manufacturer (or to a trading company or agent). For orders involving an intermediary, "first sale" valuation uses the factory-to-intermediary price as the basis for duty assessment instead of the intermediary-to-buyer price. This requires commercial documentation linking the factory invoice to the import entry and is not available in all transaction structures. A licensed customs broker can advise on eligibility.

**Duty drawback.** If the packaging is used in manufacturing a finished product that is subsequently exported from the US, the importer may recover up to 99% of the duties paid through a duty drawback claim filed with CBP. This is well-established for packaging: a cosmetics or spirits brand that imports rigid gift boxes from China, fills them at a US facility, and then exports the finished product to European retailers can recover nearly all the duty paid on the empty packaging. Documentation linking the imported packaging to the exported finished product is required.

**Foreign Trade Zones.** Goods held in a US [Foreign Trade Zone](https://www.trade.gov/foreign-trade-zones-program) do not trigger duty payment until they enter US commerce. Brands with significant inventory or re-export requirements can defer duty through an FTZ. The FTZ must be approved by the Foreign-Trade Zones Board; major US distribution hub cities have established FTZs.

**Tariff engineering.** In some packaging categories, buying greyboard and wrapping stock as flat raw materials (classified under Chapter 48 base materials at a lower rate) rather than assembled set-up boxes can reduce the applicable tariff. This requires legal counsel review; it is not universally available and requires that the assembly step genuinely occur outside China.

## How do Huamei's certifications affect US import compliance?

Certifications do not reduce tariff rates, but they satisfy the due-diligence requirements of US retail buyers and compliance programmes. Huamei holds BSCI, CE, [EQS](https://www.eqs.com/), [FSC](https://fsc.org/en), and SGS certifications. For US retail accounts that require social-audit documentation (BSCI audit results) or forest-stewardship chain-of-custody (FSC) as conditions of supplier approval, these certifications are required documents that must accompany the import paperwork.

Huamei's factory energy mix — >80% solar generation — is a supply-chain ESG data point that US importers can include in their own Scope 3 emissions reporting or in ESG disclosures to retail buyers. This is increasingly a condition of supplier approval for major US and European retail accounts, separate from tariff treatment.

## Is sourcing luxury packaging from China still cost-effective under current tariffs?

At Huamei's confirmed MOQ of 200+ pieces and 15–20 day production cycle, the total landed cost of luxury custom rigid packaging from China — even at elevated Section 301 tariff loads — is typically competitive with US domestic production. The US does not have a domestic manufacturing base for custom luxury rigid boxes at the quality level required for premium spirits, cosmetics, and gifting brands. Custom hot-foil, registered deboss, and magnetic-closure rigid boxes at Huamei's tier of finish are not available domestically in the US at equivalent unit economics.

The [Wuliangye Premium Brew clamshell](/volumes/wuliangye-clamshell) and the range of structures in Huamei's 99-structure library represent capability that is concentrated in China. For US brands, the sourcing decision turns on landed cost modelling, not on tariff rates alone.
